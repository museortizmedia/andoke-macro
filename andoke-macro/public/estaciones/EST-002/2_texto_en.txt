# HOUSE, TERRACES, AND JADE

Before we continue, take a look at the house in front of you. Remember the tent we mentioned earlier? This was the home that came next. Some of its glass and other materials had a previous life: they were salvaged and reused. The intention wasn’t to build a perfect house, but to learn how to live in this place while causing as little impact as possible. Here, conservation didn’t begin as an idea. It began as a way of life.

Now, begin walking slowly downhill and notice the terraces that follow the shape of the mountain. Take a closer look at the walls. Stone upon stone. Their construction is inspired by ancient Indigenous knowledge and a technique known as dry-stone construction, in which stones are carefully fitted together without the use of cement or mortar.

There is something beautiful about this way of building: instead of imposing a shape on the mountain, you learn to work with it. The stones are carefully arranged, allowing water to drain through while helping support the soil, creating terraces that follow the natural slope of the land. At Andoke, we wanted to recover some of that wisdom: first observe the land, understand it, and only then find a way to inhabit it.

As you make your way down, take another look at those walls. What may seem like a simple stone pathway also holds an ancient way of relating to the land.

As you continue downhill, look for the jade vine. When it blooms, its blue-green color is hard to forget. In its native habitat in the Philippines, its flowers are pollinated by bats. Yes—bats. When we think of pollinators, we usually picture bees or butterflies. But nature has many other workers.

And if you find any fallen jade flowers, take a closer look at their shape. Here at Andoke, we discovered that when you place two of them together, they look like the wings of a butterfly. And that is how our “Andokian earrings” were born.
