# THE CARIBBEAN, SIERRA NEVADA, AND LA GUAJIRA

We have been following the water from the mountains. We watched it begin, flow downhill, and become great rivers. Here, the Cauca River joins the Magdalena, and the Magdalena continues its journey until it reaches the Caribbean Sea.

Listen. Here, the story of the land is also told through dance. The Barranquilla Carnival brings together music, dances, characters, and traditions born from the encounter of different cultural heritages.

Now, look up. Facing the Caribbean, the land rises once again. This is the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta. Imagine the contrast: very close to the warm waters of the Caribbean, the mountains rise to more than 5,700 meters above sea level. Tropical sea and high mountains, almost face to face.

But the Sierra cannot be understood only through its height. For the Arhuaco, Kogui, Wiwa, and Kankuamo peoples, it is an ancestral, living, and sacred territory. And here, we want to stop speaking. For a few moments, we invite you to listen to a voice from the Sierra.

Around the Sierra is a network of sacred sites known as the Black Line — Línea Negra, connecting the mountains with the sea. Here, a mountain can be much more than a physical feature of the landscape. It can be territory, memory, knowledge, and life.

Continue eastward to La Guajira — Where Every Drop Matters. The vegetation begins to change. The desert meets the sea. We are arriving in La Guajira, the ancestral territory of the Wayuu people. After following so much water throughout our journey, here its presence—and also its absence—takes on a different meaning. In a land shaped by long periods of drought, every drop matters.

Think about what we have just traveled through: rivers, the Caribbean Sea, a mountain range rising more than five thousand meters, and desert. All within the same region. This, too, is Colombia: a country capable of changing worlds within a remarkably short distance.
